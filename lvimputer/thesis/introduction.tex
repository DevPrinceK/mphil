\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Background}
Environmental monitoring systems now produce streams of readings at a pace that would have been awkward to manage only a few years ago. A single sensor can emit measurements every few seconds or minutes, and a modest deployment quickly becomes a long sequence of temperature, humidity, light, and voltage values that must be stored, aligned, cleaned, and interpreted. That scale is useful, but it is not tidy. Real sensor data are often interrupted by communication dropouts, battery failures, calibration drift, or brief hardware faults, and the result is a series with gaps that may be small, irregular, or unexpectedly long. Missing values are rarely the only issue either; corrupted readings and obvious spikes can sit beside them, turning the observed signal into a mixture of genuine measurements and error rather than a simple incomplete record \cite{Adhikari2022ACS,Agbo2022MissingDI,Osman2018ASO}.

The importance of this problem has grown with the spread of Internet of Things (IoT) systems. Sensors are now used in agriculture, smart buildings, industrial monitoring, environmental surveillance, transport systems, and health-related applications. In these settings, data are not collected merely for storage; they often support forecasting, anomaly detection, automated control, calibration, or operational decisions. When the underlying readings are incomplete, the downstream analysis may become unstable or biased. A temperature curve with missing segments may hide a sudden environmental change. A voltage series with gaps may fail to reveal sensor degradation. A humidity signal with long missing blocks may weaken a forecasting model. For this reason, imputation is not just a cosmetic data-cleaning step; it is part of the reliability of the whole analytical pipeline.

Missing-data imputation has therefore become a broad research area that spans statistics, machine learning, deep learning, and domain-specific sensor modelling. Recent reviews show that the field contains many competing approaches, from simple constant replacement to complex generative models, but also that no single method is consistently best across all datasets and missingness conditions \cite{Adnan2022ARO,Alwateer2024MissingDI,Fan2025AnIA,Miao2023AnES}. This is especially true for sensor time series because the temporal structure of the signal matters. A value is not only a number; it is also a point in a sequence, affected by what came before it, what comes after it, and sometimes by seasonal or daily patterns. Effective imputation must therefore respect both numerical accuracy and the shape of the reconstructed signal.

\subsection{Why missingness matters}
The difficulty is not just that values are missing, but that the mechanism behind the missingness may matter. Classical missing-data theory separates missing completely at random (MCAR), missing at random (MAR), and missing not at random (MNAR). These categories describe whether the probability of a value being missing is unrelated to the data, related to observed variables, or related to the missing value itself \cite{Little2019,Jamshidian2014,Pham2022}. The same imputation method can behave quite differently across those settings. A method that performs well when gaps are randomly scattered may be unreliable when missingness is caused by sensor faults, environmental extremes, or communication failures.

Sensor data often falls somewhere between these categories. A network interruption may remove a block of readings regardless of the actual temperature, which resembles a technical missingness mechanism. A battery or voltage failure may be related to device behaviour and therefore to other observed variables. A sensor exposed to unusual environmental conditions may fail exactly when the true reading is most important, making the missingness more informative. In practice, the analyst often cannot fully observe the mechanism that produced the gap. This uncertainty makes it important to evaluate imputation methods under multiple controlled missingness scenarios instead of relying on one random mask.

Missing values also matter because many downstream algorithms assume complete inputs. A forecasting model, clustering algorithm, or calibration routine may either reject missing values outright or silently convert them into misleading patterns. Simple deletion is rarely attractive for time series because removing timestamps can destroy temporal spacing and reduce the continuity of the signal. Imputation therefore becomes a compromise: the missing value is not truly recovered, but a defensible estimate is inserted so that analysis can proceed. The quality of that estimate determines whether the completed series remains useful.

\subsection{Patterns in time-series gaps}
Time-series imputation is especially sensitive to the pattern of missingness. Filling a one-step gap in the middle of a smooth curve is not the same task as reconstructing the first samples of a series, where there is no left-hand context at all. The same applies to longer gaps: interpolation may look acceptable when the missing interval is short, yet it can flatten peaks or miss a sharp change when the gap widens. Studies on IoT and industrial sensor data show that gap size, gap location, and the available temporal context can materially change the performance of both statistical and learned methods \cite{Wu2022DataIF,Ahmed2022LongGM,Toye2025BenchmarkingMD}.

For this reason, the present study does not evaluate missingness as one generic condition. It separates the problem into START, MIDDLE, END, and RANDOM missingness. START missingness tests whether a method can estimate the beginning of a sequence without previous observations. END missingness tests whether a method can extrapolate or exploit broader seasonal context near the end of the series. MIDDLE missingness tests whether a long internal block can be reconstructed using both past and future information. RANDOM missingness tests performance when many small gaps are scattered through the signal. These scenarios are deliberately simple, but they expose different weaknesses.

The pattern of missingness also affects visual interpretation. A curve may obtain a low average error while still looking physically implausible in a local segment, especially if it creates sudden jumps or overly smooth transitions. Conversely, a method may preserve the general shape but perform poorly on a few extreme readings, increasing RMSE. This is why the study combines numerical metrics such as MAE and RMSE with graph-based inspection. For sensor imputation, the reconstructed line should not only be close to the ground truth; it should also behave like a plausible continuation of the observed signal.

\section{Motivation}
This thesis focuses on a univariate temperature series, but that narrower scope does not make the problem trivial. Temperature readings are often smoother than other sensor channels, which may suggest that simple interpolation is enough. Sometimes it is. Yet a smooth daily curve can still hide abrupt changes caused by weather shifts, heating or cooling cycles, sensor drift, or rare measurement faults. A method that blurs everything can produce a plausible line and still miss the local structure that matters. That tension is one reason imputation remains a useful research problem rather than a solved preprocessing step.

The literature has moved in several directions at once. Simple methods such as mean, median, forward fill, and interpolation remain common because they are easy to explain, easy to reproduce, and inexpensive to compute. Machine-learning methods, including K-nearest neighbours, regularised regression, random forests, gradient boosting, and iterative regression-based imputers, may perform better when the signal has a learnable structure \cite{Raj2023MachineLB,Erhan2021CriticalCO,Lee2025ComparisonOM}. Deep approaches, especially those built around recurrent networks, autoencoders, variational autoencoders, or adversarial training, often promise stronger reconstructions on difficult gaps, though that promise depends heavily on the dataset, masking regime, and evaluation design \cite{Yoon2018GAINMD,Wang2024MissingDI,Shahbazian2023GenerativeAN,Okafor2021}.

Those trade-offs are not abstract. In IoT and environmental monitoring, an imputation method has to be accurate, stable, explainable enough to justify to a reviewer or supervisor, and affordable enough to run on modest hardware. A method that improves RMSE by a small amount but requires a large training cost may still be useful in an offline study, yet less attractive if the same pipeline must later support live data. By the same token, a very simple method may be appealing for deployment and still fail to preserve the local shape of the series. Comparative studies repeatedly show that imputation performance depends on the data source, the gap pattern, the missingness rate, and the evaluation protocol \cite{Koshta2021ACO,Farjallah2025EvaluationOM,Toye2025BenchmarkingMD}.

The motivation for this work is therefore practical as well as methodological. The goal is not merely to build a complex neural model, but to test whether a sequence model can provide a meaningful advantage over simpler baselines under a controlled and transparent protocol. If the learned model improves only under some missingness patterns, that outcome is still valuable because it clarifies when the additional modelling effort is justified. If a simple baseline remains competitive under random gaps, that too is important because it prevents unnecessary complexity. The thesis therefore treats baseline comparison as central, not as an afterthought.

Another motivation is the need for models that are fair to strong baselines. Deep learning models can appear impressive when they are compared only with weak constant imputers, but a sensor time series with smooth dynamics can make interpolation and lag-based machine-learning methods surprisingly strong. A convincing evaluation must therefore include statistical baselines, machine-learning baselines, and visual inspection. This study adopts that broader comparison so that the proposed LSTM-VAE is judged against methods that a practitioner might reasonably use.

\section{Problem Statement}
Missing data in IoT sensor streams remains a practical and methodological problem because the best imputation method is rarely obvious before evaluation. Simple statistical methods are easy to apply, but they can ignore the temporal structure of the signal. Interpolation and forward-fill methods can perform well on smooth short gaps, but they may fail when missing values occur at the boundaries of the series or across long continuous blocks. Machine-learning methods can learn from lagged patterns, but their performance depends on feature design and on whether enough relevant context remains available. Deep learning methods may capture richer temporal structure, but they can also introduce unnecessary complexity if they are not tested against strong baselines \cite{Adhikari2022ACS,Erhan2021CriticalCO,Toye2025BenchmarkingMD}.

The problem addressed in this thesis is therefore not simply the existence of missing temperature readings. Rather, it is the uncertainty about which imputation strategy gives the most reliable reconstruction under different missingness patterns. A method that performs well for randomly scattered missing values may not perform as well for missing blocks at the start or end of a sequence. Similarly, a model that reduces average error may still produce a visually weak reconstruction if it distorts the local shape of the time series. This creates a need for an evaluation framework that considers both quantitative accuracy and graph-based inspection.

Another part of the problem is fairness of comparison. Deep imputation models are sometimes presented as improvements over very simple baselines, but in sensor time series, strong baselines such as linear interpolation, KNN regression, gradient boosting, and iterative regression-based imputation can be difficult to beat. Without comparing against these methods under the same masks and metrics, it is hard to know whether a proposed deep model is genuinely useful. This thesis addresses that gap by evaluating a hybrid residual LSTM-VAE against statistical and machine-learning baselines using shared missingness scenarios, shared ground truth, and masked-point MAE and RMSE.

\section{Research Aim and Objectives}
The aim of this thesis is to examine missing-data imputation for an IoT temperature time series using a controlled comparison between statistical baselines, machine-learning baselines, and a hybrid residual LSTM-VAE model. The study introduces synthetic missingness into a real sensor sequence, uses the original values as ground truth, and evaluates imputed values only at the hidden positions. This gives a direct way to measure whether the proposed method improves reconstruction accuracy and visual plausibility.

The proposed model is based on a Variational Autoencoder with LSTM components because recurrent networks are naturally suited to ordered sequences, while VAEs provide a latent representation that can support reconstruction under uncertainty. However, the study also recognises that a neural network does not need to generate the full signal from nothing. The implemented method uses deterministic priors, such as interpolation and seasonal KNN matching, and lets the LSTM-VAE learn residual corrections. This hybrid design reflects the practical nature of the problem: when a simple method is already strong, the deep model should refine it rather than ignore it.

The study also aims to make the experimental pipeline reproducible. The same missingness masks are reused across methods, random seeds are fixed, and all methods are evaluated with the same metrics. This matters because imputation results can change substantially when masks, preprocessing choices, or evaluation points differ. A method should not appear better simply because it was tested on an easier set of missing values. The research design therefore emphasises consistent masking and masked-point evaluation.

\subsection{Research objectives}
The work is guided by the following objectives:

\begin{itemize}
	\item prepare a reproducible pipeline for loading, sorting, cleaning, and masking the sensor data;
	\item construct multiple missingness scenarios that represent boundary, internal-block, and random gaps;
	\item compare standard statistical imputers with machine-learning baselines under the same missingness patterns;
	\item implement a hybrid residual LSTM-VAE that combines deterministic priors with sequence-based residual learning;
	\item evaluate the methods using masked-point error metrics such as MAE and RMSE;
	\item compare the methods visually to determine whether the reconstructed curves preserve plausible temporal structure;
	\item measure imputation-only runtime so that accuracy can be considered alongside computational cost.
\end{itemize}

These objectives are intended to connect implementation with interpretation. The numerical results identify which methods reduce error, while the plots show whether those reductions correspond to sensible reconstructed signals. The runtime comparison adds a practical dimension, since a method that is accurate but expensive may not be suitable for all deployment contexts. Together, the objectives support a balanced evaluation rather than a single-metric comparison.

\subsection{Research question}
The central research question is direct: when the same temperature series is corrupted in the same way, does the hybrid LSTM-VAE produce a better reconstruction than the statistical and machine-learning baselines, and under what kinds of gaps does that advantage appear most clearly?

This question has two parts. The first part asks whether the proposed model improves quantitative accuracy. This is answered using MAE and RMSE at masked positions. The second part asks where the improvement occurs. This is answered by separating the evaluation into START, MIDDLE, END, and RANDOM missingness. The distinction matters because the best method may not be the same across all gap types. A model can be strong at boundary reconstruction but unnecessary for short random gaps, or it can improve RMSE while producing a curve that is visually less convincing.

The research question is also intentionally comparative. The thesis does not assume that deep learning is automatically superior. Instead, it asks whether the added modelling complexity is justified in this setting. That framing makes negative or mixed findings meaningful as well. If a simple method wins under a particular gap pattern, the result helps define the practical limits of the proposed model. If the hybrid model wins across most or all comparisons, the result strengthens the case for combining prior-based imputation with residual sequence modelling.

\section{Research Methodology (Brief)}
This study uses an experimental and comparative research design. A real IoT temperature dataset is first loaded, sorted chronologically, and prepared as a univariate time series. The original observed readings are kept as the ground truth for evaluation. Synthetic missingness is then introduced so that the true hidden values remain known. This makes it possible to measure imputation accuracy directly rather than judging the filled values only by appearance.

The experiment uses four missingness scenarios: START, MIDDLE, END, and RANDOM. Each scenario hides 30\% of the sequence, but the location and structure of the missing values differ. START hides the beginning of the series, MIDDLE hides a continuous block in the centre, END hides the final part of the series, and RANDOM hides scattered indices using a fixed seed. These masks are deliberately simple, but they test different kinds of temporal context. A method that performs well when missing points are scattered may not behave the same way when a long boundary block is removed.

Several baseline methods are applied to the same masks. The statistical and time-aware baselines include mean, median, mode, random sampling, hot-deck sampling, LOCF, LOCB, forward fill, and linear interpolation. The machine-learning baselines use lag-based features and include KNN regression, ridge regression, histogram gradient boosting, and an IterativeImputer with Bayesian Ridge. These baselines are included because the proposed model should be compared with methods that are simple, reproducible, and reasonably competitive.

The proposed approach is a hybrid residual LSTM-VAE. It first constructs a deterministic prior using interpolation for MIDDLE and RANDOM gaps and seasonal KNN matching for START and END gaps. The LSTM-VAE then learns residual corrections around this prior rather than reconstructing the entire signal from scratch. A conservative residual gate is used during inference so that the neural correction is applied only when validation behaviour supports it. Model performance is evaluated using MAE and RMSE at masked positions, visual reconstruction plots, baseline win rate, and imputation-only runtime.

\section{Significance of the Study}
This study is significant because missing sensor data can affect the reliability of the analyses built on top of IoT systems. Environmental monitoring data may be used for forecasting, anomaly detection, calibration, reporting, or decision support. If missing readings are filled poorly, later conclusions may reflect artefacts of the imputation method rather than the behaviour of the monitored environment. By studying imputation carefully in a real sensor series, the thesis contributes to the broader effort to improve data quality in IoT applications \cite{Agbo2022MissingDI,Osman2018ASO,Alwateer2024MissingDI}.

The study is also significant because it compares methods across different gap structures instead of treating missingness as one uniform condition. This matters for practical deployment. A field sensor may lose a few isolated readings, an entire block of readings, or the beginning or end of a recording period. A useful imputation strategy should be understood in relation to these scenarios. By evaluating START, MIDDLE, END, and RANDOM missingness separately, the thesis shows where different methods are likely to succeed or fail.

Methodologically, the work is significant because it combines strong baselines with a hybrid deep learning approach. The residual LSTM-VAE is not evaluated in isolation; it is compared with statistical methods, time-aware methods, and lag-based machine-learning methods. This makes the conclusions more credible, because the proposed model must improve over alternatives that are simple, interpretable, and often competitive. The inclusion of imputation-only runtime also makes the study more practical, since computational cost is part of whether a method is useful outside a notebook.

Finally, the study provides a reproducible workflow that can be extended. Although the current work focuses on one univariate temperature series, the same experimental structure can be applied to additional sensors, multivariate datasets, different missingness rates, or real missingness events. In that sense, the thesis offers both a specific evaluation of a hybrid LSTM-VAE and a reusable template for comparing imputation methods in sensor-data research.

\section{Scope and Contributions}
The scope is deliberately narrow. The work concentrates on a single temperature channel from a real sensor dataset so that the comparison remains interpretable and the effect of each imputation method stays visible. This choice excludes multivariate sensor fusion, spatial sensor relationships, irregular sampling, streaming deployment, and fully online learning. Those extensions are important, but including all of them would make it difficult to isolate the central question of whether a hybrid LSTM-VAE improves univariate time-series imputation under controlled missingness.

The single-channel scope also has methodological advantages. Because the model cannot rely on neighbouring sensors or correlated variables, the experiment focuses on temporal structure within the series itself. This makes it easier to compare simple interpolation, lag-based machine learning, and LSTM-based sequence modelling. It also keeps the qualitative plots readable: the reader can inspect one reconstructed temperature curve rather than a high-dimensional multivariate output.

Within that boundary, the thesis makes several contributions. First, it sets out a fair experimental protocol in which all methods face the same masks and are evaluated against the same ground truth. Second, it compares statistical, machine-learning, and deep-learning imputers in one workflow, reducing the risk that differences are caused by inconsistent preprocessing. Third, it implements a hybrid residual LSTM-VAE that uses deterministic priors as a foundation and learns neural corrections only where useful. Fourth, it evaluates not only error metrics but also graph depictions and imputation-only runtime.

The contribution is therefore not a claim that one architecture solves missing data in all settings. Rather, it is a focused empirical study of how a hybrid sequence model behaves on a real temperature series under several gap structures. The value of the work lies in the combination of controlled masking, strong baselines, reproducible implementation, and practical interpretation. This makes the study useful both as a thesis experiment and as a foundation for later work on multivariate or real-time sensor imputation.

\section{Delineation and Assumptions of the Study}
The study is delineated to the imputation of missing values in a single IoT temperature time series. It does not attempt to solve every missing-data problem in IoT systems. The focus is on reconstructing missing temperature readings under controlled gap patterns and comparing the proposed hybrid LSTM-VAE with selected statistical and machine-learning baselines. This boundary keeps the work manageable and makes the results easier to interpret. A broader multivariate study would be useful, but it would also introduce extra questions about cross-sensor relationships, feature selection, spatial correlation, and sensor-specific noise.

The work is also limited to an offline experimental setting. The model is trained and evaluated after the dataset has been collected. It is not implemented as a live streaming imputer on an edge device or microcontroller. Runtime is measured for the imputation step, but the study does not claim to optimise memory use, energy consumption, or online retraining. In that sense, the thesis evaluates the modelling idea first, leaving deployment engineering for later work.

Several assumptions guide the experiment. First, the chronological ordering of the dataset is assumed to be correct after sorting by timestamp. Second, the observed values used as ground truth are treated as the reference values for evaluation, even though a small number of extreme artefacts are cleaned for neural-model training. Third, the synthetic masks are assumed to be useful approximations of common missingness patterns, even though real sensor failures may be more complicated. Fourth, MAE and RMSE are assumed to provide meaningful measures of imputation accuracy at the masked positions. These metrics do not capture every visual or operational property of a reconstructed signal, so the study also uses graph-based inspection.

Finally, the study assumes that a fair comparison requires shared masks, shared evaluation points, and consistent metrics. The proposed model, statistical baselines, and machine-learning baselines are therefore evaluated under the same missingness scenarios. This does not remove every possible source of methodological judgement, but it reduces the chance that one method appears better simply because it was tested on an easier version of the problem.

\section{Limitations of the Study}
Like any controlled experiment, this study has boundaries that should be acknowledged. First, the work focuses on a single univariate temperature series. This makes the comparison clear and interpretable, but it also means the results cannot automatically be generalised to all sensor types, all environmental conditions, or all IoT deployments. Other channels, such as humidity, light, voltage, air quality, or pressure, may have different noise patterns and temporal behaviours. A method that performs well on temperature may need adjustment before it is applied to a more volatile or less seasonal signal.

Second, the evaluation relies on synthetic missingness. This is useful because the true values are known and MAE/RMSE can be computed directly, but synthetic masks cannot capture every real cause of missing sensor data. In practice, gaps may be caused by battery failure, network loss, calibration drift, physical damage, or environmental extremes. These mechanisms may interact with the true value being measured, which can make real missingness more complicated than a controlled START, MIDDLE, END, or RANDOM mask.

Third, the study evaluates an offline imputation pipeline rather than a live streaming system. Training time, memory use, and deployment constraints are therefore not explored as deeply as they would be in an edge-computing or real-time monitoring application. The thesis does measure imputation-only runtime, but it does not implement online updates, continuous retraining, or embedded-device optimisation.

Finally, the proposed model is compared within one experimental setup. The findings are useful for understanding this dataset and this protocol, but broader claims would require testing on additional datasets, different missingness rates, multivariate sensor networks, and real missing events. These limitations do not weaken the purpose of the study; they define the boundary within which the results should be interpreted.

\section{Thesis Structure}
The remainder of the thesis is organised as follows. Chapter 2 reviews related work on missing-data mechanisms, time-series imputation, statistical methods, machine-learning approaches, and deep learning models for sensor data. It places the present study within the broader imputation literature and explains why no single method can be assumed to dominate across all missingness patterns.

Chapter 3 describes the methodology. It explains the dataset, preprocessing decisions, robust cleaning step, synthetic masking protocol, statistical and machine-learning baselines, hybrid prior construction, residual LSTM-VAE architecture, training strategy, inference procedure, evaluation metrics, visual comparisons, and runtime measurement. This chapter connects the research aim to the actual experimental pipeline.

Chapter 4 presents and discusses the experimental results. It reports MAE and RMSE under START, MIDDLE, END, and RANDOM missingness, compares the proposed method against statistical and machine-learning baselines, discusses the qualitative plots, and interprets what the results mean for sensor-data imputation. Chapter 5 concludes the thesis and identifies directions for future work, including multivariate extension, online imputation, and broader testing on additional sensor datasets.
